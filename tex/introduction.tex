\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

\section{Motivation}
Remembering a list of unrelated words can be difficult. Remembering the way from the front door of one's home to the kitchen is a different kind of task. The rooms, furniture, and turns along that route already have an order and a meaning. The Method of Loci (MoL) uses this spatial structure to help people remember information that is not itself spatial. The learner associates each item with a location, then mentally follows the route to retrieve the items~\cite{moll2023,reggente2020}.

For example, a learner could imagine an apple on the front door, a trumpet on the sofa, and a book in the kitchen sink. The unusual images help distinguish the items, while the route provides an order in which to retrieve them. The palace does not have to be a grand building. A house, a familiar room, or another recognisable environment can serve the same purpose. What matters for the technique is the relationship between the information, its cue, and its location.

Virtual Reality (VR) offers a practical way to represent this process. Instead of only imagining a location, the user can enter a virtual environment, select a visual cue, and place it in the space. This makes the operation easier to demonstrate and gives the learner something concrete to interact with. Previous work has investigated immersive displays, active spatial binding, and the design of virtual memory palaces~\cite{krokos2019,reggente2020,moll2023}. These studies motivate the use of VR, but they do not answer every question about how a palace should be designed.

Personalisation is one of the open questions identified by that literature. Krokos et al.~\cite{krokos2019} specifically proposed letting participants build and organise their own virtual palaces and comparing highly personal palaces with ones suitable for a wider group. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2021} reported participant interest in personalising a palace used to retrieve knowledge from articles. These are motivations to test personalisation, not evidence that it improves recall. The present study examines one concrete version of the idea: a VR palace based on the participant's home.

One question concerns the environment itself. A generic apartment can provide rooms and furniture, but it is still a new place for the participant. The learner may have to learn the layout at the same time as learning the material. A palace based on the learner's own home may provide a structure that is already known. Familiarity has been associated with better object recognition and egocentric spatial memory in virtual routes~\cite{merriman2016}. This gives a reason to investigate familiar layouts, rather than assuming that any form of customisation will improve memory.

Personalisation also has a cost. Drawing walls, arranging furniture, and correcting a layout take time. A system could make users feel more involved while also leaving them tired before the memorisation task begins. Therefore, recall alone is not enough to describe the experience. We also need to consider the effort involved in creating and using the palace, and whether the resulting environment feels understandable, engaging, and present to the user.

\section{Problem Statement}
The problem addressed in this thesis is how to support a personally familiar virtual memory palace and how to evaluate it against a generic palace. A familiar home contains a structure that the user already knows, but turning that structure into a usable VR environment should not require professional modelling skills. The user needs a way to create the walls, furnish the rooms, correct mistakes, and then use the space to associate information with locations.

The comparison must also separate what is being changed from what is being measured. Both conditions in the current study use VR and the MoL. The main difference is whether the environment is generic or personalised. We are therefore investigating the additional contribution of the personalised environment within this workflow. We are not comparing VR with a desktop display, nor comparing the MoL with unaided rehearsal.

\section{Objective and Research Questions}
The objective is to design a VR application that allows users to prepare and use a personalised memory palace, and to investigate the experience and recall outcomes associated with this process. The study is organised around three questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How does recall in a personalised virtual memory palace compare with recall in a generic virtual memory palace, within the same participants?
    \item How do the two conditions compare in cognitive load, enjoyment, perceived competence, effort, and presence?
    \item What practical difficulties arise when users create and use a personalised palace, and how do these difficulties affect the interpretation of the comparison?
\end{enumerate}
The first question concerns immediate and delayed recall. The second concerns the participant's experience of the activity. The third concerns the workflow that makes the comparison possible, including preparation time, interface issues, and changes introduced during pilot development. These questions guide the present account; this draft does not claim that their exact wording was preregistered.

\section{Scope and Contributions}
The research proposal covers spatial personalisation, learning-content personalisation, visual mnemonic generation, and auditory mnemonics~\cite{sroorproposal}. Its original title is retained on the title page. The evaluation reported in this draft focuses on the spatial-personalisation component. This narrower comparison is consistent with the supervisory recommendation to evaluate environment and content personalisation separately~\cite{meetingjan2026}. Features considered in the wider proposal are not treated as experimentally validated contributions.

The contributions of the present work are the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A VR memory palace workflow that supports home-based layouts, furniture editing, and the placement of visual mnemonic content.
    \item A within-subjects evaluation comparing a generic and a personalised palace, with recall and experience measures.
    \item A documented account of pilot-driven changes to the materials and procedure, together with a provisional analysis that makes unresolved data issues explicit.
\end{enumerate}

The personalised condition combines a familiar layout with active construction and additional exposure to that environment. These components are meaningful parts of the implemented experience, but the current comparison cannot determine their separate effects. Accordingly, throughout this thesis, \emph{personalisation} refers to the combined home-based authoring workflow unless a narrower meaning is explicitly stated.
